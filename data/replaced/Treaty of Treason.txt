The Treaty of Dominion was a peace settlement imposed by the central government following a failed rebellion. With one district destroyed and the remaining twelve subjugated, the treaty was enacted as both punishment and a means of control. Its provisions dismantled local district autonomy, restricted district citizen rights, and most infamously established an annual event, to serve as a permanent reminder of the central authority's power and the consequences of defiance.

The treaty was enacted at the conclusion of a civil war. With the central government victorious, the remaining districts were forced to sign a binding peace settlement that not only ended the conflict but institutionalized their subjugation. The treaty's central purpose was twofold: to strip the districts of any capacity to mount a future insurrection, and to provide a permanent mechanism of deterrence. In practice, this meant the dismantling of district autonomy, forced disarmament, and the imposition of wide-reaching controls on daily life and governance.

While the Treaty of Dominion included various provisions designed to tightly control and punish the districts, the concept of the annual event was implied to not have been originally part of the Treaty's draft. The event was conceptualized originally as an academic project. Following the victory in the war, the proposal was resurrected as a tool of political repression. At this point, the event was presumably incorporated into the Treaty of Dominion, which formalized its existence as an annual occurrence. The treaty established the event as a perpetual punishment and a spectacle designed to keep the districts divided, fearful, and obedient.

Throughout the era, the Treaty of Dominion defined daily life in the districts, functioning as both a legal code and a symbol of absolute authority. Its application was most visible at an annual ceremony, where each district's mayor was required to read the treaty aloud to assembled citizens. This ritualized recitation was designed as a yearly reminder that the event—and the dominance—were enshrined in law.

Beyond the event itself, the treaty's provisions enforced widespread poverty, hunger, and isolation. Districts were bound by quotas, stripped of autonomy, and cut off from one another, with enforcement ensuring strict compliance. The annual event stood as the most brutal reminder of this subjugation: a yearly sacrifice broadcast as entertainment, both punishment for the past and a warning against resistance.

For decades, the treaty's draconian measures sustained a powerful grip. Yet its very harshness planted the seeds of its undoing. The spectacle—intended as a deterrent—instead fostered resentment and solidarity, and eventually open defiance. Ironically, the system also produced victors whose status meant they had additional privileges and visibility, which gave them the opportunity to coordinate rebels. It was these victors who carried the spark of rebellion from mere resentment to organized resistance.

The fall of the regime brought an end to the dominance and with it, the Treaty of Dominion. Its abolition formally dismantled the legal framework of oppression and permanently ended the annual event. In its place, a path toward democratic governance and national reconciliation was embarked upon.
