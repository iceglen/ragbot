
"Angels, Lenore Dove told me, are humans with wings, who live in a place called heaven. Some people believe, she said, it's a possible destination after death. A good world for good people to go to."
—Haymitch Abernathy[src]
Heaven, also known as the hereafter,[1] is a domain of the afterlife believed in by some citizens of Panem. It was believed to be inhabited by angels and good people.[2] The plural form, "heavens," could also be used to describe the sky,[3][4] or as an exclamation.[5][6]

History[]
The Ballad of Songbirds and Snakes[]
There was a tradition from District 2, which Sejanus Plinth initiated after the deaths of Brandy and Marcus.  It consisted of sprinkling bread crumbs over the recently deceased while praying.[7] This tradition was to make sure the dead person had enough food on their journey "[b]ack to wherever [they] came from." The citizens of District 2 performed the ritual whenever somebody died. Coriolanus Snow felt second-hand embarrassment for Sejanus' Ma after she explained the ritual. He considered it a waste of resources and thought it an example of "primitive customs."[8]  
Three songs sung by Lucy Gray Baird mention heaven(s): the Valley Song, "The Old Therebefore," and "Lucy Gray." The Valley Song, sung in the Capitol Zoo, features the lyrics, "Birds in the heavens know I love you."[4] When Clemensia Dovecote refused to feed her tribute Jessup, her classmate Festus Creed told her, "Oh, for heaven's sake, feed him, Clemmie!"[5]

 	  	 	 			   	 	 		 			 		 		 			The Old Therebefore (Acapella) (from The Hunger Games- The Ballad of Songbirds & Snakes) 		 		 	 
"The Old Therebefore" was sung during the 10th Hunger Games, after Dr. Gaul released the snake mutts into the arena. Coriolanus Snow assumed that the song was an old one, due to it mentioning heaven. This implies that heaven was viewed as a thing of the past in the Capitol after the Dark Days. Its lyrics about "the sweet old hereafter" reminded Coriolanus of Sejanus Plinth's bread-sprinkling tradition. The speaker in the song muses about being on the threshold between the present and the afterlife, and having "loose ends to tie up" before going to heaven.[1]  
Partway through "Lucy Gray," which is a musical adaptation of Wiliam Wordsworth's poem of the same name (which is Lucy Gray Baird's Covey ballad namesake), Lucy's Father and Mother cry when they can't find a trace of Lucy in the snow. They say, "In heaven we all shall meet," and then Lucy's Mother finds her footprints. However, they are unable to find Lucy in the end.[9]

Sunrise on the Reaping[]

"“Prophet!” said I, “thing of evil! — prophet still, if bird or devil!/By that Heaven that bends above us — by that God we both adore —/Tell this soul with sorrow laden if, within the distant Aidenn,/It shall clasp a sainted maiden whom the angels name Lenore —/Clasp a rare and radiant maiden whom the angels name Lenore.”/Quoth the Raven “Nevermore.”"
—The narrator in Poe's "The Raven"[src]
Haymitch Abernathy hadn't heard of heaven before his girlfriend, Lenore Dove Baird, explained it to him. It was mentioned in her Covey name ballad, "The Raven" by Edgar Allan Poe. She said it was where angels lived, and that it was a "good world for good people to go to" after death. Haymitch thought the only winged being on his mind was Lenore Dove herself. While reminiscing during the 50th Hunger Games, he was unsure whether he would be able to be with Lenore Dove again in heaven.[2] After watching the broadcast of the fallen tributes with Maysilee Donner, he considered whether he had actually died already and what he was then experiencing was "one of Lenore Dove's worlds, where [he] continue[s] to dream of life."[10] After Maysilee died, Haymitch remarked that "she’s gone to wherever people go when they die."[11]  
Burdock Everdeen sang "The Old Therebefore" as a dirge at the funeral of Willamae and Sid Abernathy, Louella McCoy, Maysilee Donner, and Wyatt and Jethro Callow. Haymitch was consoled by the song's implication that his separation from his family and friends was "only temporary." He thought Lenore Dove would approve, too, if she hadn't been in prison. Mourners followed up the song with the three-finger salute.[12]
After the dissolution of the Hunger Games and the usurping of Presidents Snow and Coin, Haymitch started getting better, and stopped drinking out of compulsion. He believed that the goal Lenore Dove wanted to be achieved in her dying breath had finally come to fruition through Katniss after Haymitch had abandoned it decades ago. He considered Lenore Dove to age alongside him, speaking to him and guiding him. He wasn't sure how long his liver would last, and was unsure how long it would be until he moved on from "the old therebefore." He believed that nobody could ever take the woman he loved away from him.[13]

The Hunger Games[]
While Cato was mauled by wolf mutts at the Cornucopia, the only way Katniss and Peeta could determine how much time had passed was by "the heavens [skies], the subtle shift of the moon."[3]

Trivia[]
The Heavensbee family (including Trajan, Hilarius, and Plutarch), the namesake of Heavensbee Hall, likely derived their family name from the term Heaven. This might imply that some people in the Capitol still believe in Heaven and not just in the districts, or at least that their ancestors did.
The real-world folk song Down in the Valley has numerous variations, and the Valley Song is only one. Some other variations mention God and/or angels: "God and his angels know I love you"[15] or "Angels in heaven know I love you".[16]
"The Old Therebefore" contains a few lyrics that may be Biblical references.
"When I've paid all my debts" may be a reference to Colossians 2:14.
"When I've emptied my cup" likely derives from Matthew 20:22-24, and "When I'm pure like a dove" is reminiscent of Matthew 10:16 - "I am sending you out like sheep among wolves. Therefore be as shrewd as snakes and as innocent as doves."
"When I've learned how to love" bears resemblance to John 14:23 - "Anyone who loves me will obey my teaching. My Father will love them, and we will come to them and make our home with them."
Heaven is mentioned in the lyrics of multiple songs in the film series' soundtracks.
"Atlas" by Coldplay from Catching Fire - "Heaven we hope/Is just up the road."
"Devil May Cry" by The Weeknd from Catching Fire - "The light will shine through the rain/And heaven will hear them call your name"
"Bury Me Beneath the Willow" by Molly Tuttle from The Ballad of Songbirds & Snakes - "When shall I see him? Oh, no, never/'Til we meet in heaven above"
"Kingdom Come" by the Civil Wars from The Hunger Games. The phrase, "Your kingdom come" derives from the Biblical Lord's Prayer in the Gospel of Matthew, in which it refers to the Kingdom of Heaven.
